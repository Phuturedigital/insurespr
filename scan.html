The scan

Ten minutes lying down. Then you actually know.

Here is the whole thing, start to finish, without a single word you would have to look up.

Start here

What is a DXA scan?

DXA stands for dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. That is the only time you will need to read those words.

It is a scan that sends two very low-strength X-ray beams through your body. Dense material like bone absorbs them differently to muscle and fat, and the machine turns that difference into measurements. It is the same principle as a normal X-ray, at a small fraction of the strength, and it produces numbers rather than a picture of a break.

In one sentence

You lie still for about ten minutes and walk out knowing how strong your bones are and how much muscle and fat you carry, and where.

Minute by minute

What actually happens

No needles, no dye, no enclosed tunnel, no getting undressed into a gown. Genuinely.

01

You talk to a nurse first

Before anything is switched on, you go through your history — past fractures, medication you take, whether a parent broke a hip, whether you have lost height, what you want to still be able to do in ten years.

This part matters more than people expect. The same number means different things for different people, and this is the conversation that decides which.

02

You lie down on an open table

Flat on your back, on a padded table in an open room. You keep your own clothes on — you will usually just be asked to take off anything with metal in it, like a belt, a zip or jewellery.

A scanning arm passes slowly above you. It does not touch you. There is no tunnel to go into and nothing closes around you, which is the single thing people are most relieved to hear.

03

You stay still for about ten minutes

You can breathe normally and talk. Staying reasonably still is the only thing asked of you — movement blurs the reading in the same way it blurs a photograph.

Most scans cover the lower spine and one hip, because those are the two places a fracture does the most damage to your life. A whole-body scan takes a little longer and is what gives the muscle and fat breakdown.

04

Someone sits down and explains it

This is the part most people have never had. Your results come up on a screen and get talked through — what is normal, what is not, what it means for you specifically, and what can actually be done about it.

You leave with the numbers written down and a plan, plus a date to come back and check whether the plan worked.

Three answers

What you find out

The practice's own phrase for this is "more than just bone density" — because one scan answers three separate questions.

Bone

How solid your skeleton is. Measured at the spine and hip, where a break costs you the most. This is the number that says whether you are heading toward brittle bones — years before anything breaks.

Muscle

How much muscle you are carrying, and where. Muscle quietly disappears with age, and it is what catches you when you stumble. Losing it on one side only is worth knowing about too.

Fat

How much fat you carry, and where it sits. Fat around the middle behaves differently to fat elsewhere. A bathroom scale cannot tell the two apart. This can.

Body composition

What you are actually made of — rather than just what you weigh. Two people at the same weight can be built completely differently, and it changes what each of them should do next.

Reading your result

The number you will be given, and what it means

Bone results come back as a T-score. It sounds technical. It is actually just one number comparing your bones to those of a healthy young adult.

−1 or above

Normal. Your bones are about as dense as they should be. The job now is holding on to that.

−1 to −2.5

Thinner than they should be. The clinical word is osteopenia. This is the stage where there is still a great deal you can do — and the whole reason for scanning early.

−2.5 or below

Osteoporosis. Bones have thinned enough that they break more easily. It is treatable, and treatment works better the sooner it starts.

These three bands are the standard classification published by the World Health Organization and used worldwide. A score is never read on its own, though — your age, your history and your other risk factors all change what it means for you, which is exactly what the consultation is for.

The two questions everyone asks

Is it safe, and what do I wear?

The radiation

A DXA uses a very small dose — far lower than a standard chest X-ray, and a great deal lower than a CT scan. It is low enough that the scan is used routinely for repeat monitoring over many years.

If you want the exact figure for the machine at the practice, ask when you book. Any radiography service will tell you, and a good one will offer before you ask.

What to wear

  • Comfortable clothes with no metal — avoid zips, studs, underwire and belts if you can.
  • Leave jewellery at home, or expect to take it off.
  • Tell them if you might be pregnant. The scan is not done during pregnancy.
  • Mention any recent scan that used contrast dye — it can affect the reading, and they may reschedule you.
  • Bring a list of your medication, and any previous bone scan results you have.

Worth booking if…

Reasons people come in

For your bones

  • You have been through menopause
  • A parent or sibling has broken a hip, or has osteoporosis
  • You have broken a bone from a fall that should not have broken one
  • You have lost height, or your posture has changed
  • You take, or have taken, steroids long term
  • You live with a condition that affects bone — kidney disease among them

For everything else it measures

  • You train seriously and want real numbers instead of a scale
  • You are changing how you eat and want to see what is actually changing
  • You keep picking up the same injury on the same side
  • You have noticed you are less steady on your feet than you were
  • You are recovering from illness and want to know what you lost
  • You simply want a baseline while everything is still fine

Still wondering

Questions people ask

Will it hurt?

No. Nothing touches you and nothing goes into you. The most uncomfortable part is lying still on a firm table for ten minutes.

Do I have to get undressed?

Usually not. You stay in your own clothes. You may be asked to remove metal — a belt, jewellery, sometimes an underwired bra — because metal shows up on the scan and interferes with the reading.

Is it the same as an MRI?

No, and this is the difference that matters if you dislike enclosed spaces. An MRI is a tunnel you go inside, and it is loud. A DXA is an open table in an open room with an arm that passes above you. Nothing closes around you.

How often should I have one?

It depends entirely on what the first one finds and what you are being treated for. Bone changes slowly, so scans are usually spaced out by a year or more — repeating one too soon simply cannot show a real change. The practice will tell you what interval makes sense for you.

Will my medical aid cover it?

That depends on your plan and on why you are being scanned — cover is often better where there is a clinical reason such as a previous fracture or a diagnosis. Ask the practice directly, and check with your scheme before you come in.

Can I bring someone with me?

Yes, and it is often a good idea. A second person hears the explanation too, and remembers the half of it you will not.
